# Deck narrative structure — modern reference

Pitch decks live and die on structure. Every source below has published either book-length method (Duarte, Dunford) or public talk (Raskin, Laja) documenting what works. Distilled to the moves that survive across sources.

Use as scaffolding when a deck feels flabby, or when a slide won't die and you can't say why.

---

## Andy Raskin — Strategic Narrative

The reference framework for B2B pitches. Cited by First Round, YC, and every senior product marketer since 2018.
- Source: [Andy Raskin's Medium archive](https://medium.com/@andyraskin), [The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen (Zuora case study)](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0)

### The five moves

1. **Name a big, undeniable shift in the world.** Not a trend — a shift. Something the reader already believes is happening.
2. **Show that there will be winners and losers.** The stakes must be real.
3. **Tease the promised land.** Show the future state for those who navigate the shift correctly.
4. **Introduce the "magic gifts" you provide** to help them win.
5. **Show evidence you can deliver** (case studies, proof, credibility).

### The load-bearing rules within the framework

**"Name the shift. Not the brand."**
- Bad opener: *"SHXFT is a studio that…"*
- Good opener: *"MENA activation budgets have doubled. The measurement layer hasn't."*

**"Name the enemy. Not a competitor."**
- The enemy is a worldview, not a company. "The old model of assuming intent" — not "Awesome Reality."
- Never mention competitors by name. Ever. Even implied competitor references date the deck fast.

**"Contrast drives narrative. Every slide oscillates between what is and what could be."**
- Application: for every slide, ask: does this slide sit on the "what is" side or the "what could be" side? If it's neither, cut it. If it's both, split it.

**"The magic gifts are the product. Don't lead with them. Lead them to them."**
- Application: hero slide = shift. Slide 2-3 = stakes. Slide 4 = promised land. Slide 5-onward = product/gifts. Never open with "here's the platform."

## Nancy Duarte — Sparkline / Resonate

The presentation-design canon. Distilled from *Resonate* (2010) and 15 years of executive coaching.
- Source: [Duarte's Resonate book](https://www.duarte.com/resonate/), [Sparkline framework](https://www.duarte.com/sparkline/)

### The Sparkline

Every great presentation oscillates between:
- **What is** — the current reality the audience knows
- **What could be** — the future state you're proposing

The audience emotional journey follows the oscillation. Slides that stay in "what is" bore. Slides that stay in "what could be" feel unmoored. Great decks alternate.

### The load-bearing rules

**"Audiences don't care what YOU want to say. They care what THEY need to hear."**
- Application: before writing a slide, ask: what does the reader need to hear at this point in the narrative? Not: what do I want to say at this point?

**"End on a Big Idea, not a summary."**
- Application: never close a deck with a recap of what you covered. Close with the single sentence you want the reader to carry into the meeting tomorrow. That's the Big Idea. Everything else supports it.

**"The audience is the hero. You are the mentor."**
- Same principle as TBWA and Raskin. Different framing.
- Application: "You'll…" beats "we'll…" in the CTA slide. Frame the promised land as the reader's future, not the vendor's offering.

## April Dunford — Obviously Awesome

The positioning canon since 2019. Reference for anyone writing SaaS/B2B copy.
- Source: [Dunford's Obviously Awesome book](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome), [Sales Pitch book (2023)](https://www.aprildunford.com/salespitch)

### The five components of a positioning statement

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what the buyer would use if you didn't exist
2. **Unique attributes** — features/capabilities only you have
3. **Value** — the benefit those attributes deliver
4. **Ideal customer** — the specific buyer who cares most about that value
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference you fit in

**Application to decks:** every slide should serve one of these five. If a slide doesn't advance any of them, cut it. If a slide serves two, split it.

### The load-bearing rules

**"Density scales with reader control."**
- A poster: 7 words. A landing page: 15 words + subhead. A deck slide: layered — the reader can pause, scroll, revisit.
- Application: deck slides can carry more than a landing page but should still respect the reader's time. Never write a slide with more words than the reader would tolerate reading aloud.

**"Position before you write copy."**
- Application: pre-write checklist. Can you answer, in one sentence each: "For whom?" and "Vs. what?" If not, no copy will land because you don't know what claim you're making.

**"The sales pitch is the positioning, delivered live."**
- 2023 rule from Dunford's Sales Pitch book.
- Application: the deck IS the positioning. It's not a document that references the positioning. Every slide reinforces one of the five components.

## Peep Laja / Wynter — Message clarity

The copywriting-testing school. Reference for anyone writing landing pages or sales collateral.
- Source: [CXL blog](https://cxl.com/blog/), [Wynter methodology](https://wynter.com/blog/), [Laja's YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@peeplaja)

### The load-bearing rules

**"Clarity beats cleverness. Every time."**
- Application: if the reader has to work to understand the sentence, the sentence has failed. Clever wordplay that requires re-reading is a self-indulgence, not craft.

**"The 5-second test."**
- After 5 seconds on a slide, can the reader tell (a) what it is, (b) what it does, and (c) why they should care?
- Application: for every slide, run the 5-second test with someone outside the project. If they fail, the slide fails.

**"Buyers don't buy what they don't understand."**
- Application: no matter how brilliant the strategy, if the deck copy is unclear, the deal dies. Clarity is a business KPI, not a stylistic preference.

**"Test the hero. Then test the body."**
- Application: iterate the hero H1 first. Get it right, then work on body. If the H1 doesn't land, no body copy will save the slide.

## First Round Capital — Portfolio deck advice

The most-read source for early-stage pitch-deck advice, 2018-2026.
- Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/), [The deck template](https://review.firstround.com/the-first-page-of-the-deck-should-say-what-you-do/)

### The load-bearing rules

**"The headline of every slide should carry the argument. Not the section label."**
- Bad slide title: *"Market Overview"*
- Good slide title: *"MENA activation budgets doubled. Measurement didn't."*
- Application: no slide should have a label-title. Every title should make a claim the body then proves.

**"Ruthless page allocation."**
- Every extra slide dilutes the memorable ones.
- Application: if a slide could be merged with the one before or after, merge. If a slide is there because "we should probably cover this," cut it. The deck should be as short as the argument allows.

**"Show, don't tell."**
- Application: for every claim, ask: can I show this instead of stating it? A stat visualisation beats "we grew massively." A screenshot beats "the product is intuitive." A quote beats "clients love us."

## The distilled deck-narrative operating model

Combining Raskin + Duarte + Dunford + Laja + First Round, the shape that consistently wins:

### The 8-slide skeleton

1. **The Shift** — an undeniable change in the world (Raskin)
2. **The Stakes** — winners and losers, named (Raskin)
3. **The Tension** — the gap between what is and what should be (Duarte)
4. **The Insight** — the observation that reframes the tension (SHXFT house rule)
5. **The Promised Land** — the future state for those who move now (Raskin)
6. **The Gifts** — what we bring, framed as capability (Raskin + Dunford)
7. **The Proof** — case studies, stats, credibility (Raskin + Dunford)
8. **The Big Idea + Next Step** — one sentence the reader takes with them + one concrete action (Duarte + First Round)

Every SHXFT/CNQR deck should map cleanly to these 8 beats. Some beats can occupy 2-3 physical slides; some can be compressed. But the 8-move arc should be visible in the deck's underlying skeleton.

If you can't trace the 8 beats, the deck has a narrative problem — not a copy problem. Fix the structure before rewriting the words.

---

## The final rule that Raskin, Duarte, Dunford, Laja, and First Round all agree on

> **Start with the reader's world tilting. End with their next move.**

Everything between is proof, texture, and specificity. Never open with the brand. Never close with a recap. Open with the shift; close with the step.

That single rule, applied consistently, is what separates senior-agency deck writing from junior deck writing. It's what makes the deck feel like the reader's story instead of ours.
